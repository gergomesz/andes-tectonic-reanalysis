Reading the Puna

A reanalysis of H. Daxberger’s mountain-front indices, southern Central Andes

Puna (Quechua): ‘the high, cold land’—a tableland some four thousand metres above the sea, where the air is thin enough that Andean Spanish uses the same word for the altitude sickness it brings, and for the cold wind that comes down off its ridges.

Faults push rock up. Rivers and weather wear it down. Where the faults are winning, mountain fronts stay straight and valleys stay narrow. Where erosion has caught up, both get worn back. Measure those shapes from satellite elevation data and you can map which faults are still moving, without ever dating a rock. Heidi Daxberger did this for 189 mountain fronts across the southern Central Andes. This site rebuilds her three indices from first principles, then re-runs her published numbers.

Mountain-front sinuosity

How straight is the edge of a mountain?

Graph 2 · in simple terms

Smf compares how long a mountain front is when you follow every bend with how long it is in a straight line. An active fault keeps the front straight; a quiet one lets rivers carve it into bends. Values from 1.0 to 1.65 indicate a straight, active front (Class 1). Values from 1.66 to 2.5 indicate moderate activity (Class 2). Values above 2.5 indicate a worn, inactive front (Class 3).

Smf = 1.10
Class 1 · Holocene activity
Lmf 748 · Ls 680 (drawing units)

Mechanism. A reverse fault lifts the range along a straight line. Each time it slips, the front is refreshed along that line. Between slips, streams cut back into the range and carve embayments. The front's length grows while the straight-line distance stays fixed.

Daxberger's classes: 1 for Smf ≤ 1.65 (Holocene faults), 2 for 1.66–2.5 (late Neogene to Holocene), 3 above 2.5 (Neogene and older). Bull & McFadden's classic cut-off for "active" is 1.4.

Smf = Lmf / Ls

Lmf is the length along the piedmont–range junction, Ls the straight length. A curve can never be shorter than its chord, so Smf ≥ 1. Fronts were picked where hillslope steepens past 12.5°, in segments over 5 km long.

Valley shape

Is the valley a V or a U?

Graph 3 · in simple terms

Vf compares the width of a valley floor with the depth of the valley. Where the ground is rising, the river cuts down faster than it widens, leaving a narrow V. Where the ground is stable, the floor widens into a U. Values below 0.5 indicate a V-shaped valley and active uplift; values above 1.7 indicate a U-shaped valley and stable ground.

Vf = 0.40
V-shaped

Mechanism. A river has a fixed amount of energy to cut down and to cut sideways. If the ground under it keeps rising, nearly all of that energy goes into cutting down, which leaves a deep, narrow V. If the ground is still, the river has spare energy to widen its floor into a U.

Her bins: V-shaped at Vf ≤ 0.5, intermediate up to 1.7, U-shaped above 1.7. She sampled each valley every 150 m upstream and used the median, because the values were rarely normally distributed.

Vf = 2Vfw / [(Eld − Esc) + (Erd − Esc)]

Vfw is valley-floor width, Eld and Erd the elevations of the left and right divides, Esc the valley-floor elevation.

Basin symmetry

Which way does the river lean, and why?

Graph 4 · in simple terms

T measures how far a river has been pushed away from the middle of its valley. If a fault tilts the ground, the river moves toward the lower side. T = 0 means the river runs down the middle; T = 1 means it runs along the edge. If many valleys lean the same way, a tilt is likely. If they lean in random directions, the cause is harder and softer rock instead.

T = 0.38
Mean vector points N
9 transects, 150 m apart

Mechanism. If a fault tilts a basin sideways, the main stream migrates down the tilt, away from the midline. T measures how far it has moved, from 0 (centred) to 1 (against the divide). T is a vector: it also has a direction.

Turn the tilt down and the noise up. The individual vectors stay long, but they point every which way, and their mean shrinks toward zero. That is what Daxberger found in the Andes.

T = Da / Dd

Da is the distance from the basin midline to the active channel, Dd from the midline to the divide. Basin vectors are averaged; Curry's (1956) test asks whether the mean could have arisen from random directions.

Calibration

Does sinuosity actually track fault age?

Graph 5 · in simple terms

Of the 189 fronts, 78 sit on faults whose age is already known. If Smf works as a clock, older faults should have more curved fronts. The score ρ runs from 0 (no link) to 1 (a perfect link). Overall, ρ = 0.35, a moderate link. The Puna Plateau fits well (ρ = 0.55). The Eastern Cordillera fits poorly (ρ = 0.17), as several old faults still have straight fronts. The Pampean Ranges show a strong link (ρ = 0.66), but with only nine dated fronts it is less certain.

ρ = 0.35
All provinces · n = 78
Spearman rank correlation, p = 0.0019

The key step. Only 78 of the 189 fronts sit on faults with published ages. Daxberger checked that sinuosity rises with age on those 78, then used Smf to estimate ages for the other 111. This is supervised learning on a small labelled set.

Reanalysis. The relationship is real but moderate. Faults still active into the Quaternary have a median Smf of 1.40 (n = 46), against 1.59 (n = 32) for faults that stopped earlier (one-sided Mann–Whitney p = 0.0003). The signal is strong on the Puna and weak in the Eastern Cordillera.

All 189 fronts

So how many of the faults are still alive?

Graph 6 · in simple terms

Each dot is one mountain front, placed by how curved it is. Dots left of the first dashed line are straight and active (Class 1); dots right of the second are worn and inactive (Class 3). A front stays straight for only about 100,000 years after its fault stops. Since 126 of the 189 fronts are still straight, most of these faults have moved recently, including those on the Puna.

What it shows. 126 of 189 fronts fall in Class 1. If faulting on the plateau had stopped more than 2 million years ago, as earlier work suggested, and fault-generated fronts survive only about 100,000 years before erosion blunts them, Class 3 fronts should dominate the Puna. Only 5 of 76 do.

The Pampean Ranges stand apart: the widest spread and the most Class 3 fronts. That fits their thinner crust (about 40 km, against more than 60 km under the plateau) and far smaller total shortening (under 20 km).

Valleys versus fronts

Why do the valleys disagree with the fronts?

Graph 7 · in simple terms

Each dot is one mountain front. Active fronts should have both straight edges and narrow valleys, so the dots were expected to rise from lower left to upper right (dashed arrow). Instead they fall (solid line): the most active fronts have the widest valleys. The cause is not yet known. The satellite data may be too coarse to see narrow valley floors, or sediment may be filling the valleys.

ρ = −0.36
Spearman, 189 fronts
p < 0.001

Expected. Bull & McFadden's framework predicts that active fronts have both low Smf and narrow, V-shaped valleys, so the points should climb from lower left to upper right.

Empirical Observations. Per-front medians run the other way. Class 1 fronts have a median Vf of 1.10 and Class 3 fronts 0.73. The thesis notes that Vf does not track fault age, and makes its positive case from map patterns rather than from these medians.

Candidate reasons. Vf is sampled along each whole tributary, not just at the front. A 90 m DEM resampled to 30 m blurs narrow floors. Active plateau basins fill with sediment, which widens floors. Each is testable.

One point per front: median Vf of its tributary basins against its Smf. The solid line is a least-squares fit in log space; the dashed arrow is the direction the classic model expects.

Tilt directions

Is there a tilt hiding in 3,354 basins?

Graph 8 · in simple terms

Each diagram shows which way the rivers lean in one region; bigger petals mean more rivers leaning that way. The valleys run east to west, so the rivers can only lean north or south. In every region the split is about half and half. With neither side winning, the leaning comes from harder and softer rock, not from a tectonic tilt.

Reading the roses. Petal area is proportional to the number of basins whose stream is pushed toward that bearing. The petals point north and south because the tributary basins run roughly east–west, off north–south mountain fronts. So the real question is whether north or south wins.

Neither does. The split is close to 50:50 in every province. A typical basin is noticeably lopsided (mean |T| ≈ 0.2), but the lopsidedness cancels out across basins. That supports Daxberger's reading that local rock strength sets basin asymmetry, not tectonic tilting.

One discrepancy. The thesis reports province-average T of 0.04, 0.03 and 0.01. The vector mean of the tabulated per-basin values gives 0.017, 0.020 and 0.005. The order and the conclusion are the same; the averaging step may differ.

Caveat. Neighbouring basins share rocks and faults, so they are not independent samples. The p-values here therefore understate the true uncertainty.

Data: thesis Table 4.A1, mean T-vector per basin. Hollow-tipped arrow: mean vector of all basins in the province, drawn at 20× scale so it is visible.

First-order basins

What do the big valleys remember?

Graph 9 · in simple terms

Alluvial fans are piles of gravel that form where a steep stream leaves the mountains. Where the mountain beside a basin is rising, the basin tips away from it and the main river is pushed to the far side. Where the ground is sinking along a fault, the river moves toward it. The river's position therefore shows what the fault is doing.

Method. For 32 large intermontane basins, Daxberger read the alluvial-fan profiles and the position of the main river across the basin. An undisturbed fan is smoothly concave. Uplift at the range front steepens the fan head, the stream trenches into it, and the main river is pushed away from the rising block. A normal fault does the opposite and drags the river toward it. In most basins the inferred tilt points away from Class 1 and Class 2 fronts, which links fan tilt to hanging-wall uplift on reverse faults. Counts are the basins she lists for each type; one basin can show more than one type along its margins.

What it means

Now what?

Graph 10 · in simple terms

In a simple model of a mountain range, new faults form only at its outer edge, and the faults behind them stop moving. The Andes do not follow this model. Active faults are found throughout the interior, because old weak zones in the rock keep breaking under pressure. The range is therefore still being pushed up from the inside.

Her conclusions

  1. Mountain-front sinuosity works as a proxy for fault age where no radiometric dates exist.
  2. Quaternary-to-recent deformation on bivergent reverse and thrust faults is pervasive across the Puna, Eastern Cordillera and Pampean Ranges, more than earlier work recognised.
  3. Deformation did not simply migrate to the foreland. It continues out of sequence in the hinterland.
  4. Second-order basin asymmetry (T) reflects variable local erosion, not tectonic tilt. Climate has limited influence on first-order landforms.

What the reanalysis adds

  • Smf–age: Spearman ρ = 0.35 across 78 dated fronts (p = 0.002); ρ = 0.55 on the Puna, 0.17 in the Eastern Cordillera.
  • Front-scale Vf correlates negatively with Smf (ρ = −0.36), opposite to the classic expectation (sheet 07).
  • T directions split close to 50:50 north–south in all three provinces (sheet 08).
